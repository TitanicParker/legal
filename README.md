# legal
In July 2017 an integrated feet–gait–tone presentation generated, on Neurology's own examination, both severe young-onset Parkinsonism and a dystonic-looking lower-limb phenomenon alongside genuine structural foot disease. The final specialist synthesis allowed the painful foot/leg phenomena to be separate or neuropathic and ultimately “Not clear.” The surviving record demonstrates substantial neurological work, but it does not clearly demonstrate the focused discriminative examination that would show how those competing lower-limb mechanisms were separated. The central question is whether that work occurred and, if not, what reasonable practice required at the time.

That should now be the controlling formulation of the 2017 case. It is designed to do something much more precise than allege that the diagnosis was wrong. It identifies the clinical relationship that required resolution, the point at which that relationship became a neurological question, the way the final synthesis organised the uncertainty, and the factual gap that has to be answered before any conclusion about breach can safely follow.

The chronology underneath it is strong and does not depend on hindsight. Primary care documented bilateral painful and abnormal feet together with abnormal gait; then movement-initiation difficulty; then increased tone; then sent the feet, gait and muscle-tension problem into Neurology as an integrated presentation. During admission Neurology found severe rigidity/bradykinesia, a striking gait, a lower-limb appearance suggestive of dystonic posture, and structural foot abnormalities. Only later does the portable specialist account leave the foot/leg relationship as potentially separate or neuropathic and “Not clear.”

The formulation deliberately accepts three things that make it stronger rather than weaker. It accepts that the Parkinsonian syndrome was genuinely and extensively investigated. It accepts that the structural foot disease was real. And it accepts that “Not clear” could have been a perfectly proper medical conclusion. Our complaint is therefore not uncertainty itself. It is the unresolved question of what clinical work converted an integrated feet–gait–tone presentation into a formulation in which those components might properly be treated as separate. The project's evidential rules already capture that discipline: structural pathology does not establish structural independence; Podiatry may be appropriate without resolving neurological ownership; and “Not clear” proves neither adequacy nor inadequacy by itself.

Duty

The duty theory should not be framed as a duty to diagnose PRKN, or even necessarily a duty to diagnose dystonia in July 2017.

It is narrower: once the clinical presentation itself raised competing explanations for the same lower-limb phenomenon, reasonable neurological assessment had to address that relationship to an appropriate clinical level.

The reason the duty arises from the contemporaneous facts is important. Neurology was not simply presented with corns and callouses. The service was presented with abnormal gait, increasing tone and movement-initiation difficulty, and then itself observed a lower limb that looked sufficiently unusual to be described as dystonic-looking rather than true foot drop. At the same time, pes planus and hammertoes were present. That combination creates an ordinary neurological discrimination problem: is the observed lower-limb abnormality weakness, rigidity, dystonic posturing, pain compensation, structural restriction, neuropathic, or mixed?

The relevant duty therefore concerns the quality of the discrimination, not achievement of diagnostic certainty. The necessary work may have included relatively basic bedside matters: sufficient distal motor examination to support “not foot drop”; enough sensory/reflex assessment to give clinical content to a neuropathy possibility; observation of whether the foot/toe posture was fixed or dynamic; its behaviour with movement; and some attempt to understand the relation between structural deformity, loading, posture and pain. The final synthesis itself creates the internal comparator because it shows high-resolution reasoning about the Parkinsonian syndrome while the surviving record shows substantially less resolution around the abnormal lower limb. The project describes this as the possibility that “the synthesis may have outrun the examination.”

That does not mean Neurology had to treat corns or assume the functions of Podiatry. A proper division of labour could have been entirely reasonable. But somebody within the neurological pathway still had to own the neurological arrows: weakness versus dystonic posturing, central motor contribution versus structural restriction, and the basis—if any—for neuropathy. That is why referral to Podiatry can be both appropriate and insufficient to answer the neurological relationship question.

The Protest, if proved, creates a second and separate duty point. It should not be used to rewrite the preceding admission. If immediately before discharge the patient said “you have not dealt with my feet,” the question becomes what reasonable closure required once the patient expressly indicated that the original disabling problem remained unfinished. If an adequate relationship-directed examination had already occurred, the answer may simply have been explanation and a defined follow-up pathway. If it had not occurred, the same words become notice while the patient was still available for clarification or re-examination. Our architecture correctly keeps those two questions separate.

Breach

The breach theory follows directly from that duty, but it must remain conditional.

The allegation is not:

They wrote “Not clear,” therefore they were negligent.

Nor is it:

They did not diagnose dystonia, therefore they were negligent.

The candidate breach is:

If reasonable practice required a focused clinical discrimination of the feet–gait–tone relationship, was that discrimination actually performed before the relationship was allowed to settle as separate/neuropathic/“Not clear”?

That framing matters because the current record leaves a genuine evidential fork. There is positive evidence that substantial lower-limb assessment occurred: direct gait observation, video, recognition of possible dystonic posture, the explicit “NOT FOOT DROP” judgment, reflex examination, structural-foot observations and multidisciplinary involvement. We should preserve all of that.

But those facts do not conclusively establish the particular discriminators that would answer the relationship question. A gait video can show dynamic movement but cannot establish sensation or passive correctability. “NOT FOOT DROP” strongly suggests some clinical discrimination, but it does not tell us what distal power actually was. General reflex wording does not establish ankle-specific findings. This is why the breach issue remains ascertainable rather than assumed.

If records or testimony establish that the necessary examination occurred and was adequate, the initial July breach theory may narrow or fail. That is not a defect in our formulation. It is one of its strengths: it identifies the fact capable of proving or disproving the allegation.

The breach architecture then has independent later routes. Even if July 2017 was reasonable, new evidence after discharge could create a fresh obligation to revisit the original boundary. The project already separates the initial assessment, decision after notice, handoff, 2017/18 reopening, later updating and 2023 safety questions precisely because one does not have to prove every stage to establish another.

Harm

The harm case should likewise be kept narrower than “he had Parkinson's and painful feet for years.”

Neither the underlying PRKN disease nor genuine structural foot disease is itself harm caused by an alleged failure in 2017. Those conditions existed independently of clinical care.

The potentially attributable harm is the additional burden caused by failure, if proved, to characterise, integrate or subsequently revisit the lower-limb problem when reasonable care would probably have done so.

That potentially includes prolonged avoidable pain; impaired walking and standing; occupational and functional effects; missed or delayed access to a more appropriate combination of neurological and mechanical management; and, at later stages, the consequences of continuing to manage the problem through separated explanatory streams rather than as a mixed or interacting clinical problem.

The longitudinal record establishes that the foot problem was not trivial. It persisted across years and by 2023 was associated with severe bilateral foot/lower-leg pain, substantial functional restriction, serious psychological-endurance language, balance concerns and observed stumbles. But the project deliberately keeps that 2023 state analytically separate from the founding allegation: it proves the seriousness of the later harm state, not that all of that harm was caused by what happened in July 2017.

That distinction is crucial. We need eventually to separate:

baseline harm — pain and disability that would have occurred because of PRKN disease and structural foot pathology even with ideal care;

from

incremental avoidable harm — pain, disability, treatment delay or other consequences that probably would have been avoided or reduced if the relevant breach had not occurred.

That is the damages question the case ultimately has to answer.

Causation

Causation is where the later evidence becomes extremely important—but not by travelling backwards and proving the 2017 duty or breach.

The counterfactual chain must be explicit:

If the required relationship-directed examination had occurred, what would it probably have shown?
If it had shown a neurological, mechanical or mixed lower-limb mechanism, what would probably have changed in management?
If management had changed, which later harms would probably have been avoided or reduced?

That sequence is already embedded in the project's causation architecture.

The later evidence helps answer different parts of that chain.

The 2017–2018 treatment-state evidence is particularly valuable because it crosses the boundary soon after discharge. There was substantial neurological improvement after treatment; during interruption, tension increased, the feet became restless and walking deteriorated; and the treating neurologist later recorded that the outside-left-foot phenomenon was the first thing to change rapidly during withdrawal. None of that proves that a corn was caused by dystonia or that every painful foot symptom was neurological. It does establish something more restrained and useful: a phenomenon previously left on the foot side of the boundary changed with neurological treatment state.

That later information has two causal uses. First, it supports biological plausibility that earlier relationship-directed assessment might have discovered clinically meaningful interaction. Second, even if the original July uncertainty was reasonable, it strengthens the separate argument that the original boundary later became due for reconsideration.

The Podiatry evidence operates in the opposite direction and is just as important. Recurrent fifth-MTPJ pathology, clawing and offloading demonstrate a real mechanical object. That prevents us from collapsing the case into “the feet were really neurological all along.” Instead it supports the more realistic causal model: the eventual answer may be structural, neurologically influenced, or mixed. The project correctly asks whether two specialties acquired complementary truths without those truths being globally recombined.

The later OFF/dystonia and dopaminergic-response evidence adds further crossings of the original boundary. By 2022–2025, the record contains persistent morning foot limitation, OFF/dystonia reasoning, pain and shuffling treated as OFF-related, persistent toe curling, and later acknowledgement that Sinemet improves the feet/pain. This does not prove what a 2017 examination would necessarily have found. It does make it increasingly difficult to regard the relationship as clinically irrelevant, and it may assist with the counterfactual question of whether neurological recognition and treatment could have altered at least part of the later symptom burden.

The 2026 PRKN evidence has a still narrower role. It must not be used to say that clinicians in 2017 should have known the molecular diagnosis. That would violate the temporal firewall. Its proper role is biological plausibility: the eventually identified disease is one in which lower-limb dystonia is a recognised feature and may precede fuller Parkinsonian manifestations. That later natural history is therefore concordant with—but does not prove—the possibility that some component of the earlier gait/posture/tone syndrome and the later overt Parkinsonism belonged to one evolving neurological phenotype. It is much weaker evidence that the corns, callouses, pes planus, hammertoes or pain themselves were necessarily neurological.

That distinction gives the genetics real evidential value without contaminating the duty analysis.

How the whole case now works

The case is therefore no longer:

Neurology diagnosed Parkinson's when this was really dystonia and therefore everything that followed was wrong.

That would be medically crude and evidentially vulnerable.

It is:

An integrated lower-limb problem entered Neurology. Neurology correctly identified a major Parkinsonian syndrome but also observed structural foot disease and a dystonic-looking lower-limb phenomenon. The final formulation organised those elements into partially separate categories while leaving their relationship unresolved. The central 2017 question is whether sufficient clinical discrimination occurred to justify that organisation. If it did not, the next question is what would probably have been discovered and managed differently. Independently, later treatment-state, mechanical, movement-state and genetic evidence repeatedly crossed the original boundary and may have created fresh occasions on which reasonable care required the relationship to be reconstructed. The harm and causation case then asks what part of the subsequent pain, disability and treatment course would probably have been avoided or reduced had that occurred.

That gives each evidential period its proper job.

2017 contemporaneous evidence establishes the duty question and potentially the first breach.

The Protest, if proved, establishes a separate decision-after-notice route.

2017–2018 treatment-state evidence tests whether the original boundary should have been reopened.

Podiatry establishes the reality and precision of the mechanical component.

Later OFF/dystonia and medication-response evidence shows repeated neurological crossing of that supposedly separate boundary.

2023 establishes a serious later harm/safety state and potentially an independent contemporary duty issue.

2025 helps test treatability, clinical consequence and the downstream effect of the inherited explanatory frame.

2026 genetics supplies retrospective biological concordance, not retrospective duty or breach.

That is why this formulation is the right centre of gravity. It allows duty, breach, harm and causation to be proved separately rather than making them all depend on one sweeping claim that the 2017 diagnosis was wrong. It also means the case can survive mixed findings: July could be judged reasonable while a later failure to reopen was not; some pain could be structurally inevitable while another component was avoidable; Podiatry could have been appropriate while neurological integration was deficient.

The proposition we ultimately need to establish is not that every foot symptom had one cause. It is that the relationship mattered clinically, required ownership, and may have had consequences when it remained unresolved.
