# legal

## Controlling formulation

In July 2017 an expressly integrated feet–gait–tone presentation reached Neurology. On Neurology's own examination there was severe very-young-onset Parkinsonism, a striking footdrop-like gait appearance described as “somewhat suggestive of a dystonic posture — NOT FOOT DROP,” and genuine structural foot disease; the formal DAT-scan indication then carried forward that the walk was “almost dystonically unusual.” The final specialist synthesis nevertheless allowed the painful foot/leg phenomena to be “completely separate,” perhaps neuropathic, and ultimately “Not clear.”

The central 2017 question is therefore **not whether a clinically meaningful neurological lower-limb relationship was merely possible**. The contemporaneous record positively raised one. The unresolved question is whether that relationship was adequately characterised and integrated with the genuine structural pathology before the working allocation was exported, what discriminative examination actually occurred, and what reasonable neurological practice required at the time.

This is deliberately narrower than alleging that the Parkinsonism diagnosis was wrong, that all foot pain was dystonia, or that uncertainty itself was negligent. It is also stronger than an absence-of-documentation case. The record contains affirmative neurological signals that required interpretation; what remains fact- and expert-dependent is whether the work needed to interpret them was adequate.

## Why the 2017 problem was integrated

Primary care did not send Neurology an already-separated case of Parkinsonism plus podiatric disease. The chronology developed from bilateral painful abnormal feet and abnormal gait, to movement-initiation difficulty, to increased tone, inability to relax and toe clawing, with worsening functional and occupational consequences. The GP expressly sought Neurology's opinion on the patient's “gait, feet and ‘muscle tension’ issues.”

During admission Neurology identified marked rigidity/bradykinesia and a striking gait, but also did something more specific: it rejected ordinary foot drop as the explanation for the appearance and described the lower limb as potentially dystonic. Pes planus and hammertoes were present at the same time. The DAT-scan indication preserved the same phenomenological observation by describing the walk as “almost dystonically unusual.”

That is the factual centre of the case:

> **A neurologically meaningful lower-limb relationship was positively raised by the contemporaneous record. The issue is whether it was adequately characterised and integrated with the real structural pathology.**

## The synthesis–examination differential

The 2017 neurological work was substantial and must be credited. Gait was directly observed and videoed; tone and reflexes were examined; the pseudo-footdrop appearance was recognised; extensive investigation occurred; the admission was prolonged; sophisticated very-young-onset and recessive/genetic reasoning was developed.

That positive evidence prevents any fair formulation from saying that Neurology simply “ignored the feet.”

But it also creates an internal comparator. The surviving specialist record is unusually detailed and says the longer admission allowed the team to “get a lot done,” “explore a lot of questions,” and gain a “firmer footing.” Against that level of detail, the record does not clearly show the complete phenotype-directed bridge concerning distal power, sensory findings relevant to neuropathy, ankle-specific reflexes, fixed versus dynamic/passively correctable foot or toe posture, and the relationship between structure, loading, posture and pain.

Absence of a documented manoeuvre does not prove that it was not performed. “NOT FOOT DROP” may itself imply meaningful motor discrimination. But the comprehensiveness of the surrounding specialist account makes the missing bridge **more evidentially notable**, not less. The question remains:

> **What clinical work earned the separation?**

Or, more precisely:

> **Was the resolution of the lower-limb phenotype congruent with the sophistication and consequence of the final specialist synthesis?**

## Duty

The duty theory is not a duty to diagnose PRKN in July 2017, nor necessarily a duty to diagnose dystonia.

It is narrower: once the presentation and Neurology's own examination raised competing explanations for the same lower-limb phenomenon, reasonable neurological assessment had to discriminate that relationship to an appropriate clinical level.

The relevant competing mechanisms included weakness, rigidity, dystonic posturing, pain compensation, fixed structural restriction, neuropathy and mixed mechanisms. The historical expert question is what examination and reasoning reasonable neurological practice in 2017 required before leaving the relationship separate, neuropathic or “Not clear.”

Podiatry was a legitimate destination for corns, callouses, pressure lesions, footwear, offloading and structural mechanics. But Podiatry could not itself determine true distal weakness versus dystonic posturing, central motor contribution, or whether a neuropathy hypothesis had adequate neurological support. A reasonable division of labour therefore does not eliminate the question of who owned the neurological relationship.

## Breach

“Not clear” is not itself the breach. It may have been a perfectly proper medical endpoint.

The candidate July breach is conditional:

> **If reasonable practice required focused discrimination of the feet–gait–tone relationship, was that work actually performed before the relationship was allowed to settle into a working allocation of separate / neuropathic / “Not clear”?**

The record supports both sides. Neurology directly observed and videoed gait, recognised possible dystonic posture, expressly said “NOT FOOT DROP,” examined tone/reflexes and recorded structural feet. Those facts may support an inference that more discrimination occurred than was itemised.

But they do not themselves establish every discriminator relevant to the final allocation. The strongest defensible documentary proposition is therefore not “the examination was not done.” It is:

> **The surviving record does not clearly demonstrate the complete discriminative bridge, and in an unusually comprehensive specialist account that absence is evidentially significant enough to require explanation rather than presumption.**

If records or testimony establish that the required examination occurred and was adequate, the initial July route may narrow or fail.

## Protest — conditional route only

If the patient proves that immediately before discharge he said, in substance, “you have not dealt with my feet,” that creates a separate decision-after-notice question. It must not be used to rewrite the preceding admission.

If adequate relationship-directed work had already occurred, reasonable closure may have required explanation and a defined follow-up route. If it had not occurred, the patient was still available for clarification or focused re-examination. The Protest adds force if proved but is not necessary to the wider architecture.

## September 2017 — an underused contemporary threshold

The post-discharge chronology contains an important intermediate threshold before the October withdrawal evidence.

In September 2017 an independent specialist, Prof Daniel Healy, wrote that he thought the patient **“probably has Park II”** and suggested testing PARK II first. He also recorded dramatic improvement in motor symptoms on procyclidine and rasagiline. At roughly the same stage, Podiatry was documenting real focal mechanical pathology while the patient reported that his gait had previously been very bad but was no longer so.

This does not retrospectively determine the July standard. It does mean that by later 2017 PARK2 was not merely one theoretical item in a differential: it had become an independently favoured contemporary hypothesis, while neurological functional change and mechanical foot pathology were being observed in parallel.

That creates its own expert question:

> **Did the emerging PARK2 phenotype and changing neurological state require renewed attention to the unresolved lower-limb relationship?**

## 2017–2018 reopening

The October 2017 procyclidine withdrawal produced a new contemporaneous observation: tension increased, the feet became restless and the walk deteriorated together. In April 2018 the treating neurologist himself recorded that the outside-left-foot phenomenon was the first thing to change rapidly during withdrawal, with the patient becoming more “shifty” and “unrelaxed.”

This does not prove that a corn was caused by dystonia or that every painful foot symptom was neurological.

It does establish something stronger than mere possibility:

> **A new neurological-treatment-state observation involved the feet and required clinical interpretation against the earlier separation hypothesis.**

Whether reasonable practice required a formal reassessment at that point remains an expert question. The clinical relevance of the signal itself is affirmative.

## Structural pathology and the missing join

The Podiatry evidence is not contrary material to be minimised. It repeatedly demonstrates genuine mechanical pathology, including recurrent left fifth-MTPJ lesions, clawing, pronation, offloading, insoles and toe-prop treatment.

The correct model is therefore not “neurological or structural.” It may be independent structural pathology, neurologically influenced structure/loading, or mixed.

The longitudinal record then contains positive information from both sides of that divide: Podiatry repeatedly localises mechanical pathology, while Neurology and the patient record treatment-state changes involving feet, gait and tension.

The cautious systems proposition is:

> **The need to integrate those streams becomes increasingly apparent; the surviving record does not clearly show a durable reconstruction of the whole relationship.**

Abstract ideas such as “information hazard,” “lock-in” or “self-validating continuity” may be useful analytical lenses, but they are not needed to carry the case. The direct clinical chronology is stronger.

## 2019–2025 — neurological relevance becomes increasingly affirmative

The later record should not be described merely as a series of speculative boundary crossings.

By 2019 primary care was again expressly relating foot problems to increased tone, abnormal gait and Parkinson's disease and recording young-onset Parkinson's with “clawing feet” and pain.

By 2023 the neurological record itself considered lower-limb ON/OFF dystonia, with OFF dystonia regarded as more likely; later leg pain and shuffling were assessed as OFF-related and dopaminergic treatment was adjusted.

By 2024 pregabalin improved foot pain while activity-associated toe curling persisted, supporting the possibility of separable components.

By 2025 the treating neurologist expressly acknowledged that the feet felt better when Sinemet took effect, predicted some additional pain relief from more Sinemet, and balanced that against the real risk of worsening dyskinesia.

These facts do not prove that the focal pain generator was dystonia. They do establish that **at least some lower-limb phenomena were being interpreted and managed as dystonic, OFF-related, dyskinetic or dopaminergically modulated by the treating service itself**.

That makes later reconstruction a progressively stronger question.

## 2023 safety

The 2023 state remains analytically separate from the founding 2017 case. The record contains severe bilateral foot/lower-leg pain, urgent GP concern, “death wish” language, “no quality of life,” mobility restriction and medication escalation, alongside genuine neurological reassessment.

The proper question is whether that combined pain–mobility–medication–psychological state was adequately recognised and managed as a contemporaneous safety object. It should not be used as proof by analogy that July 2017 care was deficient.

## Harm

Neither the underlying PRKN disease nor genuine structural foot disease is harm caused by an alleged breach.

Potentially attributable harm is only the incremental burden caused by a failure, if proved, to characterise, integrate or later reconstruct the lower-limb problem when reasonable care would probably have done so.

The later record demonstrates that the foot problem became severe and functionally consequential. It does not establish that all of that burden was avoidable.

The damages analysis must therefore distinguish:

**baseline harm** — pain and disability that would have occurred because of PRKN disease and structural foot pathology even with ideal care;

from

**incremental avoidable harm** — pain, disability, treatment delay or other consequences that probably would have been prevented or reduced absent a proved breach.

## Causation

Causation remains deliberately more cautious than the breach/reopening case.

For each breach actually supported by expert evidence, the counterfactual must be explicit:

1. What should have happened?
2. What would proper assessment probably have shown?
3. What management would probably have changed?
4. Which later harm would probably have been avoided or reduced?
5. What burden would have occurred anyway?

Treatment response is mechanistically non-specific. Podiatry proves genuine structural disease. The 2017 strategy of cautious levodopa use had a documented rationale, and later dyskinesia demonstrates a real treatment constraint. These matters must remain inside the causation analysis.

## 2026 PRKN evidence

The temporal firewall remains absolute for standard of care.

Later genetics cannot be used to say that clinicians in July 2017 should have known the molecular diagnosis, that all painful foot symptoms were dystonia, or that structural pathology was caused by PRKN.

Its proper role is nevertheless stronger than generic “biological plausibility.” CeGaT identifies pathogenic PRKN variants and describes lower-limb dystonia as a characteristic PARK2 feature that may remain isolated for years; St Vincent's later clinically attributes both Parkinsonism and dystonia to compound-heterozygous Parkin mutations.

The lawful evidential use is:

> **The eventual disease-level diagnosis positively unifies Parkinsonism and dystonia within PRKN disease, making the earlier coexistence of very-young-onset Parkinsonism and dystonic-looking lower-limb phenomenology retrospectively coherent without altering the 2017 knowledge state.**

## Final positioning

The strongest current case is therefore:

> **An integrated lower-limb problem entered Neurology. Neurology correctly identified a major Parkinsonian syndrome and, on its own examination, positively identified a dystonic-looking pseudo-footdrop phenomenon alongside genuine structural foot disease. The final synthesis placed the painful foot/leg phenomena into a working branch where they might be completely separate or neuropathic and were “Not clear.” The central July question is whether sufficient phenotype-directed clinical discrimination occurred to justify that organisation. The surviving record cannot prove that it did not, but its exceptional detail makes the missing discriminative bridge a genuine evidential issue requiring explanation and expert assessment. Independently, by September 2017 PARK2 had become an externally favoured contemporary hypothesis, and subsequent treatment-state, mechanical, OFF/dystonia, toe-curling and dopaminergic-response evidence repeatedly made the lower-limb relationship clinically salient and may have created fresh duties to reconstruct it. Later genetics supplies disease-level retrospective coherence, not retrospective breach. Harm and causation remain confined to the incremental burden that proper assessment or later reconstruction would probably have avoided or reduced.**

The proposition we ultimately need to establish is not that every foot symptom had one cause.

It is:

> **The neurological relationship was positively visible; the unresolved question is whether it was adequately characterised, owned and reconstructed when reasonable practice required it.**
