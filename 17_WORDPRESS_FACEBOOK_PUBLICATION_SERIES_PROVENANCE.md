# 17 — WordPress / Facebook Publication Series: Provenance, Contemporaneous Symptom Evidence and Memory Reliability

## Status

**Active provenance and recovery lead.**

This file concerns a series of patient-authored 2017 WordPress posts about Parkinsonism, symptoms, treatment and lived experience. The patient states that the posts were published on WordPress, shared on Facebook, and later deleted from WordPress.

The purpose of this file is not to treat publication as proof that every clinical interpretation in the posts was correct. It is to determine what the publication history can establish about **date, authorship, contemporaneous observation, dissemination, later memory and freedom from complaint-era reconstruction**.

The governing distinction is:

> **The existence and timing of a published symptom account are evidential facts separate from the truth of the medical interpretation placed on the symptoms.**

---

# 1. Surviving 4 October 2017 Gmail copy

A Gmail message sent by the patient to a family member on **4 October 2017** under the subject **“Here”** contains a long article headed **“Parkinsons Disease / Parkinsonism Lifeism.”**

The body begins with an **“Edit”** hyperlink pointing to a WordPress authoring page at `theliamo.wordpress.com`.

That is documentary support that the text was connected with a WordPress page/post rather than being created solely as an ordinary email.

The patient now states that:

- this article was subsequently published on WordPress;
- it formed part of a series of related posts;
- the posts were shared on Facebook;
- the WordPress material was later deleted.

### Evidence status

- **Gmail copy, date, authorship and WordPress edit-link:** documentary fact from surviving Gmail.
- **Publication, series, Facebook sharing and later deletion:** patient evidence, partly supported by the WordPress authoring trace but not yet fully reconstructed from native WordPress/Facebook records.

Do not silently upgrade the entire publication history to documentary fact until native or third-party traces are recovered.

---

# 2. Why publication status matters

If the 4 October article can be independently shown to have been published and shared at or near that date, the evidential status of its symptom descriptions becomes unusually strong.

It would show that the patient publicly memorialised specific bodily observations:

- within months of the July 2017 admission;
- before any complaint, litigation, governance or data-protection dispute;
- before later theories about clinical lock-in, documentary construction or negligence were developed;
- while treatment effects were still unfolding in real time.

This would not make the patient's interpretation medically correct. It would materially reduce the force of a suggestion that the **symptom descriptions themselves** were generated retrospectively for the later dispute.

The key distinction is:

> **Contemporaneous observation can be strong even when contemporaneous explanation is incomplete or wrong.**

---

# 3. “Twisting urge in my left foot”

Near the beginning of the 4 October article, while describing deterioration after stopping Kemadrin/procyclidine because of visual toxicity, the patient writes that pockets of tension were returning and describes either a ball of humming tension or:

> **“a twisting urge in my left foot”**

Later in the same article, four days into the medication break, he writes:

> **“I am feeling the tension building up, my feet are restless and my walk is falling apart”**

### Evidential importance

This is more than a generic report that the feet hurt.

It is a **foot-specific dynamic motor/sensory phenomenon**, lateralised to the left, appearing in the same treatment-state deterioration as returning bodily tension and gait breakdown.

It should not be labelled dystonia as a documentary fact. That interpretation remains medical/expert-dependent.

However, it sits close to the July 2017 clinician observation that the abnormal lower-limb posture/footdrop-like appearance was suggestive of a dystonic posture rather than true weakness.

The resulting evidential bridge is:

> **July clinician observation of dystonic-looking lower-limb behaviour → October patient description of a twisting left-foot urge during neurological medication withdrawal → restless feet + gait deterioration.**

If the October text was contemporaneously published and Facebook-shared, that bridge becomes substantially more resistant to later-hindsight criticism.

---

# 4. The article records observation and explanation diverging

The article is unusually valuable because the patient's **bodily observations** and his **causal model** are not perfectly aligned.

When recounting his older history he says, in substance, that leg tension was a separate thing from the foot problems and upper-body stiffness.

Yet in the live October medication-withdrawal portion of the same article he records:

> returning tension → twisting urge in left foot → restless feet → gait falling apart.

He also expressly says that hindsight is allowing him to piece together symptoms and that he is still figuring the condition out.

This is important because it allows later analysis to distinguish:

1. what the patient actually experienced;
2. what the patient thought the experience meant at the time;
3. what later medicine may say the phenomenon represented.

The article should therefore not be used merely as proof of the patient's 2017 theory. Its greater value may be that it preserves **raw phenomenology before the later theory was settled**.

---

# 5. Publication series may provide repeated contemporaneous state observations

The patient states that this was one of a series of WordPress articles.

If other posts survive through exports, emails, Facebook shares, caches or third-party copies, they may permit a sequential reconstruction of:

- medication-state changes;
- foot twisting/curling/restlessness;
- gait changes;
- pain language;
- visual toxicity;
- treatment response;
- evolving causal interpretation;
- what the patient did and did not understand about the feet at each stage.

A series would be more valuable than one isolated post because it could show **within-person change over time** without requiring later reconstruction.

The correct method would be:

> **post date → symptom observation → interpretation at that date → treatment state → later corroboration/contradiction.**

---

# 6. Facebook sharing matters separately from WordPress publication

The patient states that each WordPress article was shared on Facebook.

If Facebook records or data exports recover those shares, they may independently establish:

- public/share timestamps;
- title and URL of each post;
- whether the article existed at a particular date;
- audience/reactions/comments;
- possible contemporaneous third-party witnesses who read the material.

Facebook sharing would therefore be a **publication-provenance source**, not merely social-media background.

Comments or reactions should not be treated as medical corroboration unless they contain independently relevant factual evidence. Their primary value may be to prove that a particular text was in circulation at a particular time.

---

# 7. Later deletion does not erase the historical evidential event

The patient states that the WordPress material was later deleted.

If true, deletion changes present availability but does not undo earlier publication.

A deleted page can still leave historical traces in:

- Gmail;
- WordPress account/export data;
- WordPress notification or activity records;
- Facebook shares and data exports;
- browser/bookmark history;
- search-engine or web-archive captures;
- copied text sent to family/friends;
- screenshots or device backups;
- third-party comments/links.

The present absence of a live page must therefore not be equated with non-publication.

Equally, the patient's recollection of publication should not be treated as independently proven until one or more such traces is located.

---

# 8. Memory reliability: the legally safer proposition

The discovery is also relevant to the later retrospective evidence, including the alleged Protest.

The repository should **not** adopt a global proposition such as:

> “The patient's memory is reliable.”

Memory can be accurate for some matters, incomplete for others, and influenced by later information. A global character judgment is unnecessary and vulnerable.

The stronger evidential proposition is:

> **Later patient recollection can be tested for collateral accuracy against records created years earlier. Where independently checkable details are repeatedly accurate, that pattern is relevant to the weight of later recollection, while not proving any separately disputed event.**

This is a **collateral-accuracy / credibility pattern**, not bootstrapping.

---

# 9. Existing examples of collateral accuracy

The 2017 correspondence and primary clinical chronology already permit several types of cross-checking.

Examples include the patient's recollection/description of:

- longstanding severe foot problems before Neurology;
- an April 2017 return to the GP because the feet/walking problem had become intolerable;
- Podiatry referral;
- early-July hospital admission for suspected Parkinsonism;
- rasagiline and procyclidine/Kemadrin treatment;
- major early motor improvement;
- visual toxicity prompting a medication break;
- deterioration of tension, feet and gait during withdrawal;
- later continuing specialist follow-up.

Many of these matters have independent documentary counterparts in GP, hospital or later clinician records.

The proper next step is a formal **collateral-accuracy table** identifying for each later recollection:

1. the date the recollection was made;
2. the specific remembered fact;
3. the independent earlier record capable of testing it;
4. exact agreement, partial agreement, inconsistency or no comparator;
5. whether the patient likely had access to the earlier record before making the recollection.

The last question is essential. Repetition after records access is not independent memory corroboration.

---

# 10. What this does to the Protest evidence

The alleged 10 July 2017 Protest remains a disputed historical event with no contemporaneous clinical entry presently identified.

A demonstrated pattern of collateral autobiographical accuracy can legitimately affect the assessment of the patient's later recollection.

But it cannot convert the Protest into documentary fact.

The correct formulation is:

> **The Protest must be assessed on its own evidence, but the reliability of the witness's recollection can be informed by how accurately his later memory performs when tested against independent contemporaneous material.**

There is also contrary evidence to preserve: the detailed 4 October 2017 article, despite recounting the admission and much of the surrounding history, does not presently contain the Protest.

That omission is limited negative evidence. It does not negate a collateral-accuracy pattern, and a collateral-accuracy pattern does not erase the omission.

Both belong in the assessment.
