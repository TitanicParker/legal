# 06 — Terminology Provenance Audit: “Burning”, “Leg Pain” and Peripheral Neuropathy

## Status

**Active documentary audit. Not a finding of fabrication, alteration, interference, bad faith or motive.**

This document preserves a newly identified evidential problem concerning the provenance and later life of specific clinical terminology in the record. The purpose is to make the question testable before any stronger allegation is considered.

The present controlling working transcription is `REPAIRED_CANONICAL_V2.md`, subject to original-source verification. The user-supplied source image of the July history is also relevant because typography may itself become evidential if confirmed against the original document.

The central question is simple:

> **Where did the words “burning” and “leg pain” come from, and how did those terms subsequently function in the longitudinal record?**

---

# 1. The patient's recorded July vocabulary

In the July admission history, the patient's problem is recorded through a stable distinction:

- **foot pain**;
- **leg tension**;
- corns and calluses;
- fallen arches / foot problems.

The relevant passage is `CLIN-000968–000976`:

> He has had to have treatment for these feet problem since 2009 and 2010 and they remain a problem.
>
> In 2012 he left Bar Working and for the most part, this seemed to improve the **pain in the feet** which reduced to a degree “out of the picture” --- however he continued to have the **“leg tension”** and there are still corns an calluses”.
>
> Therefore in 2012 he began to realise that the **foot pain and the leg tension were not the same problem** because: as the **leg pain** settled --- he was more aware of the tension.

The final clause is conspicuous because the preceding logic is:

> **foot pain improved / settled while leg tension remained**

yet the wording changes to:

> **“as the leg pain settled”**

That creates a genuine provenance question. On the face of the immediately preceding text, “leg pain” is not the term through which the distinction has been built. The patient's recorded distinction is expressly **foot pain versus leg tension**.

The user-supplied source image also appears to show the phrase **“as the leg pain settled”** in typography visually distinct from the surrounding text. That formatting observation must be checked against the original source document and its native formatting/version history before any conclusion is drawn from it.

### Current status

- The phrase **“leg pain”** is present in the July text.
- Its fit with the immediately preceding narrative is internally awkward.
- Its provenance is presently **unresolved**.
- Possible explanations include ordinary dictation error, transcription error, editorial substitution during synthesis, wording elicited elsewhere but not preserved in the visible history, or another source/document process.
- **Insertion or interference is not presently proved.**

The evidential question is therefore:

> **Was “leg pain” actually patient language, clinician paraphrase, dictation/transcription substitution, or later document construction?**

---

# 2. “Burning” first appears in the neurologist's etiological paragraph

A search of the current repaired canonical before the July interpretive paragraph identifies no earlier occurrence of `burn` / `burning` in the preceding admission history or examination.

The term appears at `CLIN-001142–001145`:

> The leg symptoms including corns and callouses and **burning** may have been completely separate or perhaps a **secondary peripheral neuropathy** perhaps in some respect related to the significant original alcohol intake? **Not clear** as this has improved.

This creates a second provenance question.

The preceding recorded patient history describes:

- pain in the feet;
- corns and calluses;
- fallen arches;
- leg tension;
- a distinction between foot pain and leg tension.

The recorded examination describes the abnormal gait / footdrop-like dystonic appearance, pes planus, hammertoes and mild oedema. It does not, in the presently identified material, record the patient using **“burning”** or a sensory examination eliciting a burning quality.

Yet **“burning”** appears in the neurologist's own interpretive/etiological sentence immediately before the proposal of **secondary peripheral neuropathy**.

The correct present proposition is therefore:

> **The current July record does not show the provenance of “burning”. It is not identified in the preceding recorded patient history or examination and first appears in the specialist etiological paragraph in which a separate / peripheral-neuropathy branch is introduced.**

That is stronger and safer than saying the neurologist “invented” the term. The source question must be answered before attribution can be made.

---

# 3. Why the placement of “burning” matters

“Burning” is not merely decorative language in this context. It is a pain-quality descriptor capable of carrying clinical implications.

In the July sentence, the documentary sequence is:

> **corns and callouses + “burning” → possible complete separation → possible secondary peripheral neuropathy → possible relation to alcohol → “Not clear”**

The issue is not whether burning pain can occur in neuropathy. That is a medical question for expert evidence. The documentary question is more basic:

> **What source supplied the descriptor which appears in the same sentence as the neuropathy hypothesis?**

If the term came from patient history not preserved elsewhere, that source should be identified if possible.

If it was clinician-generated descriptive terminology, that fact matters because it would mean the specialist did not merely create the neuropathy hypothesis; the specialist also supplied part of the vocabulary through which the symptom was characterised within that hypothesis.

---

# 4. “Leg pain” has a later life in the record

The phrase does not remain confined to the anomalous July clause.

In the later contact record at `CLIN-002574–002580`, the patient is recorded as complaining of:

> **“severe neuropathic pain again in his feet”**

with reduced mobility and shuffling, worse at times when Sinemet has not been taken.

The subsequent clinical translation is:

> **“it appears he is being undertreated and the leg pain and shuffling is off related.”**

This is important because the later record is no longer using only the original July distinction of **foot pain** and **leg tension**. It now contains an established clinical phrase **“leg pain”**, and that leg pain is being integrated into OFF-related Parkinsonian reasoning.

No claim is made here that the later author copied the July phrase. The point is that **“leg pain” became a durable available term in the record and acquired later clinical work.**

---

# 5. “Burning” also has a later life

The current canonical contains a later urgent GP referral at `CLIN-002637–002640` describing:

> severe bilateral **“burning”** both feet and lower legs and pain

with the pain extreme at times and associated with “death wish” language.

The word therefore reappears years later in a much more severe presentation.

This does **not** prove that the 2023 GP inherited the word from the 2017 neurologist. The later pain may genuinely have been described as burning by the patient at that time. The evidential point is narrower:

> **A descriptor whose July provenance is currently unexplained later becomes explicit vocabulary for the severe feet/lower-leg pain state.**

That makes the origin of the July term worth establishing rather than treating it as an incidental adjective.

---

# 6. Peripheral-neuropathy terminology acquires a longitudinal life

The July specialist disposition introduces:

> **“secondary peripheral neuropathy”**

Later records use increasingly established neuropathy vocabulary, including:

- `CLIN-002574`: **“severe neuropathic pain again in his feet”**;
- `CLIN-002682`: **“ongoing ? neuropathic pain to both his feet/legs”**;
- `CLIN-002478–002480`: the earlier **“peripheral neuropathy question”**, Fabry/peripheral-neuropathy overlap and the statement that peripheral neuropathic symptoms are common in Parkinson's disease;
- `CLIN-002783`: **“If there is a degree of peripheral neuropathy here”**;
- `CLIN-003240`: **“peripheral neuropathic pain symptomatologies”**;
- `CLIN-003258–003258A`: possible alcohol contribution to **neuropathic symptoms** and discussion of other causes;
- `CLIN-003264–003266`: pregabalin described as treatment for **“neuropathic pain (of whatever cause)”**, alongside acknowledged Sinemet benefit for morning pain.

The important longitudinal question is not whether peripheral neuropathy was ever a permissible differential. It is:

> **How did a provisional July possibility acquire enough documentary durability to become a recurring vocabulary and management category across later years, and what later evidence was capable of reopening or displacing it?**

---

# 7. The provisional terminology genealogy

The present record supports testing the following documentary sequence:

> **patient history: foot pain + leg tension**
>
> → **conspicuous July phrase: “leg pain”**
>
> → **specialist July terminology: “burning” + possible secondary peripheral neuropathy**
>
> → **later “neuropathic pain” / “peripheral neuropathy” vocabulary**
>
> → **later “leg pain” used in OFF-related treatment reasoning**
>
> → **2023 severe bilateral “burning” feet/lower legs**
>
> → **2025 “peripheral neuropathic pain symptomatologies” / “neuropathic pain (of whatever cause)”**

This is a **genealogy for investigation**, not proof that every later term was copied from the earlier one.

The distinction is essential:

- **documentary recurrence** can be proved from the record;
- **inheritance from a particular earlier author** requires evidence;
- **deliberate insertion/interference** requires substantially stronger source and metadata evidence.

---

# 8. Important October 2017 source-status complication

The repaired canonical later summarises an October 2017 contemporaneous patient email as containing a historical reconstruction including recurrent stinging at the outside of the left foot and **“years of severe burning foot pain”** (`CLIN-001784`).

That evidence must be handled carefully.

It may show that by October 2017 the patient himself was describing historical foot pain in burning terms. But `CLIN-001784` is presently a **summary of the email**, not the verbatim phrase displayed immediately above it.

Therefore the exact original October 2017 email should be source-checked for:

1. whether the patient actually used the word **“burning”**;
2. the exact date and wording;
3. whether it describes the historical foot pain or a then-current symptom; and
4. whether there is any evidence connecting that later patient wording to the unexplained July usage.

Even if the patient did use “burning” in October 2017, that would **not answer where the word in the earlier July synthesis came from**. It would, however, be relevant to any claim about subsequent adoption or inheritance of terminology.

---

# 9. The source-formatting anomaly must be preserved

The user-supplied image of the July history is important because **“as the leg pain settled”** appears visually distinct from surrounding text.

This must not be rhetorically inflated. The correct next step is forensic documentary comparison:

- obtain the original native document or highest-quality source image;
- establish whether the typeface/style difference is real rather than rendering/OCR artefact;
- inspect the paragraph for mixed fonts, italics, pasted text, tracked changes or other style discontinuities;
- obtain document properties / creation and modification metadata where available;
- obtain version history or audit trail if the clinical system retains it;
- compare other additions/addenda in the same document for normal formatting behaviour; and
- determine whether the phrase existed in the earliest saved version.

Only that work can distinguish an innocent formatting/dictation history from later alteration.

---

# 10. Questions now requiring focused examination

The terminology issue should be investigated through explicit questions:

1. **Where did “burning” first originate?** Was it patient language, clinician elicitation, clinician characterisation, another source, or later synthesis language?
2. **Where did “leg pain” originate?** Why does the text move from the explicit distinction “foot pain” versus “leg tension” to “as the leg pain settled”?
3. Is the apparently different typography of **“as the leg pain settled”** present in the native/original document?
4. If so, what explains the formatting difference and when was that text added?
5. What contemporaneous clinical evidence supported **secondary peripheral neuropathy** in July 2017?
6. Was “burning” part of that evidential basis, or merely a descriptive term introduced alongside the hypothesis?
7. What objective neuropathy-focused examination or investigation was performed before or after that hypothesis was entered?
8. How did the expressions **burning**, **leg pain**, **neuropathic pain**, **peripheral neuropathy** and **secondary peripheral neuropathy** recur across later records, and which recurrences can be shown to derive from prior notes rather than fresh patient reports?
9. What did later clinicians understand by the word **“again”** in “severe neuropathic pain again in his feet”?
10. Did later use of neuropathy terminology influence treatment, referral, investigation or the threshold for reconstructing the feet–gait–tone relationship?

---

# 11. Evidence required before any allegation of interference

A serious allegation about record interference should not be made merely because terminology is suspicious or consequential.

The following would materially advance or weaken such a hypothesis:

- original July admission/discharge document in native form;
- document version history / audit trail;
- timestamps for creation, amendment, addenda and finalisation;
- dictation/transcription records if retained;
- raw ward notes / clerking notes from which the synthesis was built;
- original patient-history notes;
- original October 2017 email;
- Evolve/PCS document history;
- evidence showing whether formatting differences are routine in the same source document;
- evidence identifying who authored or amended each relevant phrase.

Until then, the repository position is:

> **There are genuine terminology-provenance anomalies requiring investigation. There is not yet sufficient evidence to characterise those anomalies as deliberate interference.**

---

# 12. Current control proposition

> **The July record presents the patient's history primarily as foot pain plus leg tension, yet contains a conspicuous shift to “leg pain” within that same account and later introduces “burning” for the first identified time in the specialist sentence that also proposes secondary peripheral neuropathy. Both “leg pain” and “burning”, together with neuropathy terminology, later acquire substantial clinical lives in the record. Their recurrence does not prove documentary inheritance or alteration, but it makes their original provenance a material evidential question that should now be tested against the native July document, its version history and the underlying contemporaneous notes.**

Shortest form:

> **Before asking what “burning neuropathic leg pain” eventually meant, establish who first supplied each of those words, when, and on what clinical evidence.**
