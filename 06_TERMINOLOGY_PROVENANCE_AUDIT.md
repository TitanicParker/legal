# 06 — Terminology Provenance Audit: “Burning”, “Leg Pain” and Peripheral Neuropathy

## Status

**Active documentary audit. Not a finding of fabrication, alteration, interference, bad faith or motive.**

This document preserves a material evidential problem concerning the provenance and later life of specific clinical terminology in the record. The purpose is to make the question testable before any stronger allegation is considered.

The present controlling working transcription is `REPAIRED_CANONICAL_V2.md`, subject to original-source verification. The correspondence audit in `16_CORRESPONDENCE_GENEALOGY_AND_CONTEMPORANEOUS_WITNESS.md` now adds an important October 2017 source correction.

The central question is simple:

> **Where did the words “burning” and “leg pain” come from, when did they enter the record, and how did those terms subsequently function in the longitudinal case?**

---

# 1. The patient's recorded July vocabulary

In the July admission history, the patient's problem is recorded through a stable distinction:

- **foot pain**;
- **leg tension**;
- corns and calluses;
- fallen arches / foot problems.

The relevant passage at `CLIN-000968–000976` says in substance that the foot pain improved after leaving bar work while the leg tension continued, and then contains the phrase:

> **“as the leg pain settled”**

The phrase is conspicuous because the immediately preceding logic is:

> **foot pain improved / settled while leg tension remained**

not:

> **leg pain settled while leg tension remained.**

That creates a genuine provenance question.

### Current status

- `leg pain` is present in the July text;
- its fit with the immediately preceding narrative is internally awkward;
- its exact provenance remains unresolved;
- plausible innocent explanations include dictation error, transcription error, clinician paraphrase, editorial substitution during synthesis, or wording elicited elsewhere but not preserved in the visible history;
- deliberate insertion or interference is not proved.

The evidential question remains:

> **Was `leg pain` actually patient language, clinician paraphrase, dictation/transcription substitution, or later document construction?**

---

# 2. “Burning” first appears in the July specialist etiological paragraph within the surviving record

A search of the repaired canonical before the relevant July interpretive paragraph identifies no earlier occurrence of `burn` / `burning` in the preceding admission history or examination.

The term appears at `CLIN-001142–001145` in the sentence that combines:

> **corns and callouses + burning → possible complete separation → possible secondary peripheral neuropathy → possible alcohol relation → Not clear.**

This creates a documentary provenance question because the preceding recorded patient history is framed in terms of foot pain, corns/calluses, fallen arches and leg tension rather than a recorded sensory descriptor of burning.

The correct proposition remains:

> **Within the presently surviving July record, the provenance of `burning` is not shown before the specialist etiological paragraph in which a possible separate/peripheral-neuropathy branch is introduced.**

That is not the same as saying the neurologist invented the term.

---

# 3. October 2017 correspondence materially corrects the stronger version of the burning hypothesis

A source-checked private patient email dated **4 October 2017** uses `burning` repeatedly and unambiguously in the patient's own voice, including the description of feet as **“scorching hot and filled with burning pain.”**

This is important contrary evidence.

It establishes that by October 2017:

- `burning` was authentic patient vocabulary;
- the patient used it in a private narrative not written for a complaint, regulator or litigation process;
- a later theory that the descriptor could only have been clinician-generated is untenable.

### What this does not establish

The October email is later than the July synthesis.

It therefore does **not** answer:

- whether the patient used `burning` during the July admission;
- whether the July author elicited it directly;
- whether it came from another clinical source;
- whether it was introduced during later synthesis;
- whether the October vocabulary itself was influenced by prior clinical discussion.

The July provenance question therefore survives, but in a narrower and more disciplined form:

> **`burning` is demonstrably patient language by 4 October 2017. What remains unresolved is how and when that same descriptor entered the earlier July specialist synthesis.**

---

# 4. The October email also changes the patient-language genealogy

The 4 October 2017 narrative says the patient had previously blamed the feet for the onset of his broader difficulties and that only **“very recently”** had he begun to understand the foot problems as another aspect of his Parkinsonism.

That evidence matters because the patient's explanatory model was already changing quickly after diagnosis and treatment.

The patient cannot therefore be represented as having held one fixed causal theory from 2017 to the present.

The relevant genealogy is more complex:

> **pre-specialist painful-feet/gait problem → post-diagnosis reinterpretation of feet through Parkinsonism → later neuropathic/separate-foot understandings → later mixed/integrated reconstruction after further treatment response and record access.**

This does not prove that the July specialist account caused the October reinterpretation. It shows only that explanatory influence and patient understanding evolved in real time.

---

# 5. “Leg pain” has a later life in the record

In later material the phrase `leg pain` is used in OFF-related treatment reasoning.

That means `leg pain` became a durable available term in the clinical record.

The evidential question is not whether later authors were forbidden to use it. It is whether its later recurrence represents:

- fresh patient report;
- clinician paraphrase;
- ordinary clinical vocabulary;
- or inheritance from the earlier narrative.

Those possibilities must be distinguished before repetition is treated as corroboration.

---

# 6. “Burning” also has a later life

The urgent 2023 GP referral describes severe bilateral **burning** of the feet/lower legs with extreme pain and safety concern.

That later use is fully compatible with genuine current patient description.

The October 2017 email now shows that burning was already authentic patient vocabulary years earlier.

Therefore the later recurrence of `burning` should no longer be framed as suspicious merely because the July provenance is unresolved.

The remaining documentary question is narrower:

> **What was the source of the July use, and what evidential work did that descriptor perform in the contemporaneous neuropathy hypothesis?**

---

# 7. Peripheral-neuropathy terminology acquires a longitudinal life

The July specialist disposition introduces **secondary peripheral neuropathy** as a possibility.

Later records use increasingly established neuropathy vocabulary, including severe neuropathic pain, ongoing possible neuropathic pain, the earlier peripheral-neuropathy question, possible alcohol contribution and pregabalin as treatment for neuropathic pain of whatever cause.

The important longitudinal question is not whether peripheral neuropathy was ever a permissible differential.

It is:

> **How did a provisional July possibility acquire documentary durability, and what later evidence was capable of increasing or reducing its weight?**

---

# 8. The revised terminology genealogy

The strongest current genealogy is now:

> **patient history in July: foot pain + leg tension**
>
> → **conspicuous July phrase: `leg pain`**
>
> → **July specialist terminology: `burning` + possible secondary peripheral neuropathy**
>
> → **4 October 2017 private patient email independently proves that `burning` was genuinely part of the patient's own vocabulary by then**
>
> → **later neuropathic-pain / peripheral-neuropathy vocabulary**
>
> → **later `leg pain` used in OFF-related treatment reasoning**
>
> → **2023 severe bilateral burning feet/lower legs**
>
> → **2025 mixed neuropathic and dopaminergic pain reasoning.**

This is a genealogy for investigation, not proof that each later term descended from the first.

---

# 9. The October narrative is also negative evidence regarding the Protest — but only limited negative evidence

The 4 October 2017 private email is detailed and close in time to the admission. It recounts the pre-admission deterioration, hospital investigation, diagnosis and treatment in considerable depth.

No account of the alleged pre-discharge Protest has been identified in the email reviewed.

That omission is relevant because the message is detailed and not litigation-driven.

It does **not** establish that the Protest did not occur. People do not record every event even in long narratives.

The correct evidential question is:

> **How much weight should the absence of the Protest from this detailed October 2017 private narrative carry when assessing the patient's later retrospective account?**

That question should be preserved alongside, not substituted for, the patient's direct evidence of the Protest.

---

# 10. Source-formatting anomaly concerning `leg pain`

A previously supplied image of the July history appeared to show **“as the leg pain settled”** in typography visually distinct from surrounding text.

That observation must not be inflated.

The correct next steps remain:

- obtain the original native document or best available source image;
- determine whether the typeface/style difference is real rather than rendering/OCR artefact;
- inspect mixed fonts, italics, pasted text, tracked changes or other style discontinuities;
- obtain document properties, creation/modification metadata, version history and audit trail where retained;
- compare normal formatting behaviour elsewhere in the same system;
- determine whether the phrase existed in the earliest available version.

Only that evidence can distinguish innocent formatting/transcription history from later alteration.

---

# 11. Questions now requiring focused examination

1. Where did `leg pain` originate, and why does the narrative shift from foot pain versus leg tension to `leg pain`?
2. Where did `burning` first originate in the **July document lifecycle**?
3. Did the patient use `burning` during the admission, even though it is not preserved in the visible earlier history/examination?
4. Was `burning` clinician characterisation, another source, dictation/transcription language, or later synthesis language?
5. What contemporaneous clinical evidence supported secondary peripheral neuropathy?
6. What objective neuropathy-focused examination or investigation was performed before or after that hypothesis was entered?
7. Is the typography anomaly around `leg pain` present in the native/original source?
8. How did `burning`, `leg pain`, `neuropathic pain`, `peripheral neuropathy` and `secondary peripheral neuropathy` recur, and which later uses were independently generated?
9. What did later clinicians mean by `again` when describing neuropathic pain in the feet?
10. What role did neuropathy terminology play in referrals, medication and the threshold for global reconstruction?
11. What weight should be given to the October 2017 private narrative as contemporaneous evidence both for authentic burning vocabulary and against overconfident assumptions about the Protest chronology?

---

# 12. Evidence required before any allegation of documentary interference

A serious allegation about record interference should not be made merely because terminology is consequential or anomalous.

The following would materially advance or weaken such a hypothesis:

- original July admission/discharge document in native form;
- document version history / audit trail;
- timestamps for creation, amendment, addenda and finalisation;
- dictation/transcription records if retained;
- raw ward notes / clerking notes;
- original patient-history notes;
- Evolve/PCS document history;
- evidence showing whether formatting differences are routine;
- evidence identifying who authored or amended each phrase.

The 31 August 2026 Article 15 provenance request is now a live route by which some of that evidence may become available.

---

# 13. Current control proposition

> **The July record presents the patient's history primarily as foot pain plus leg tension, yet contains a conspicuous shift to `leg pain` and later introduces `burning` in the same etiological sentence that proposes secondary peripheral neuropathy. A private patient email of 4 October 2017 now independently establishes that `burning` was genuinely part of the patient's own vocabulary by that date, materially weakening any claim that the descriptor could only have been clinician-generated. It does not answer how the word entered the earlier July synthesis. The strongest surviving provenance questions are therefore authorship time, source route, the anomalous `leg pain` transition, and what contemporaneous clinical evidence supported the neuropathy branch.**

Shortest form:

> **`Burning` is authentic patient language by October 2017; July provenance is still open. `Leg pain` remains the sharper textual anomaly.**
