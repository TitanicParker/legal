# 1. CONTROLLING FORMULATION

The case begins with the problem the patient actually brought into care, not with the later legal theory and not with the later genetic answer. In early 2017 he was experiencing painful abnormal feet and an abnormal gait in the setting of a much longer history of corns, calluses and foot treatment. As his GP observed him over the following weeks, the presentation broadened: difficulty initiating movement, marked stiffness and increased tone, brisk reflexes, toe clawing, worsening dexterity and increasing interference with work and daily activity became evident. The GP therefore did not send Neurology an isolated podiatry complaint. He professionally translated the presentation into a neurological problem in which feet, gait, tone, movement and function belonged to the same open clinical picture. fileciteturn27file0L6-L28 fileciteturn30file0L72-L105

Neurology then identified a serious very-young-onset Parkinsonian syndrome. The July 2017 assessment was substantial: marked symmetric increased tone, gross bradykinesia and a striking gait were documented; the lower limb could look like foot drop but was specifically described as “somewhat suggestive of a dystonic posture — NOT FOOT DROP”; and pes planus and hammertoes were also recorded. The differential remained broad, including atypical and genetic possibilities, and the patient was discussed at a complex-case meeting and investigated extensively. fileciteturn30file1L178-L258 The formulation must therefore resist two opposite distortions. Parkinsonism was real and strongly evidenced. Structural/mechanical foot pathology was also real. The case cannot credibly be framed as though one of those domains must disprove the other.

The key unresolved feature was the relationship between them. The July specialist synthesis stated that the leg/foot symptoms, including corns, calluses and burning, might have been “completely separate,” perhaps reflecting peripheral neuropathy, and concluded “Not clear.” The note also recorded that the patient himself had historically “markedly divorced” leg tension from foot pain because the two had not always varied together. fileciteturn26file0L14-L27 That contemporaneous separation is important defence evidence. But it is equally important that “Not clear” was not a finding of proven neurological independence. It was an unresolved mixed hypothesis.

Podiatry was therefore not an irrational diversion. It subsequently documented recurrent focal mechanical disease — especially callus/HD around the left fifth MTPJ, clawed toes, pronating foot type and other local findings — and treated those problems with debridement, toe props and off-loading, sometimes with reported benefit. fileciteturn30file2L473-L528 fileciteturn30file2L595-L705 The stronger clinical question is not whether Podiatry should have existed. It is whether reasonable neurological care also required continuing ownership of the unresolved neurological relationship between painful feet, lower-limb posture, gait and treatment state while structural care proceeded in parallel.

The patient's later account adds a disputed but potentially important event. He says that immediately before discharge he expressly complained that the feet — the problem through which he understood himself to have entered the admission — had not been dealt with, using the “NCT / bald tyres” analogy and receiving a dismissive-sounding response about Podiatry. fileciteturn26file2L37-L64 This must remain **DISPUTED FACT**. The accessible contemporaneous clinical record does not establish that the exchange occurred as recalled. If proved, however, it would not prove dystonia, negligence or the need for continued admission. Its significance would be narrower: it would show that the unresolved foot problem was expressly put back before the service at the point of closure, creating an additional expert question about what response, explanation, re-examination, documentation or follow-up was reasonably required.

The case becomes stronger as a longitudinal reopening case than as a retrospective attack on the July diagnosis. In September 2017 Prof Healy independently considered PARK II probable, recommended genetic testing and recorded dramatic motor improvement on low-dose procyclidine and rasagiline, while also cautioning that PARK II patients can become dyskinetic if dopamine replacement is over-used. fileciteturn25file3L87-L118 In October 2017, during a planned break from procyclidine because of visual adverse effects, the patient contemporaneously reported that tension was rebuilding, “my feet are restless and my walk is falling apart.” fileciteturn20file1L70-L91 By 2018 the treating neurologist himself recorded that when procyclidine had been stopped the first thing changing rapidly was the outside-left-foot phenomenon, together with a more “shifty” and “unrelaxed” state. fileciteturn20file2L121-L128

Those observations do not prove that a corn was neurological, that every painful foot symptom was dystonia, or that procyclidine and levodopa have interchangeable mechanisms. They establish something narrower and more durable: at least part of the disputed foot/lower-limb state was capable of changing with neurological treatment state. That was new patient-specific information bearing on a relationship which had previously been left “Not clear.” The central breach question therefore becomes threshold-specific: at what point, if any, did accumulating evidence make reasonable neurological practice require a more explicit re-synthesis, monitoring plan, referral, or treatment recalibration?

The defence remains substantial. Dopaminergic restraint was deliberate, explained and clinically reasoned. The July plan expressly referred to very young onset and risks of dyskinesia and impulse-control problems. Those concerns later became patient-specific reality: higher Neupro doses were associated with chorea/dyskinesia and impulsive or compulsive phenomena, and the treating neurologist described management as a “tight rope” between stiffness/discomfort on one side and excessive movement or behavioural toxicity on the other. fileciteturn30file1L260-L274 fileciteturn34file0L5-L25 fileciteturn34file1L72-L88 There were also long periods of high function: in 2020 walking quality was “actually very good,” and in 2022 the patient described himself as getting on very well and walking 2.5–3 hours per day despite morning soreness. fileciteturn34file3L120-L129 fileciteturn34file2L95-L109 Any fair causation theory must account for those intervals rather than compressing 2017–2025 into one continuous injury.

The later record also defeats a theory that nothing was reconsidered. By 2020 one foot-throw phenomenon was expressly classified as Parkinsonian. In 2023 Neurology considered OFF dystonia, OFF-related amplification of pain, insufficient levodopa coverage and alternative neuropathic causes; it sought Fabry testing and attempted repeat DaT imaging when the patient questioned the diagnosis. Later nursing/consultant documentation described leg pain and shuffling as appearing OFF-related and undertreated and added Stalevo. fileciteturn30file3L771-L810 fileciteturn22file0L9-L19 These are genuine acts of reconsideration. The possible failure, if an expert identifies one, is not “never reassessed” but whether reassessment remained sufficiently integrated, timely and calibrated as the phenotype and treatment evidence changed.

By 2023 the harm picture had also transformed. The GP urgently referred severe bilateral burning foot/lower-leg pain that was at times “causing death wish,” said “I am worried about him,” and sought review before “we get into trouble here.” The Parkinson nurse separately recorded debilitating pain and “no quality of life.” fileciteturn33file2L55-L73 fileciteturn33file3L82-L94 Later Neurology documentation described a heavy psychological burden from the walking problem but also recorded positive or even “great” mood, which prevents the record from being simplified into continuous major psychiatric deterioration. fileciteturn30file3L747-L763 This creates an independent safeguarding question: what risk assessment and safety response did those statements reasonably require? The present corpus shows urgent referral and clinical escalation, but no discrete safeguarding or suicide-risk response of equivalent documentary visibility has been identified. That is a documentary absence, not proof that no such action occurred.

The pain mechanisms also remained mixed. Pregabalin substantially reduced foot pain while toe curling persisted; later the patient self-reduced pregabalin from 600 mg/day to 150 mg/day total and pain worsened. fileciteturn21file4L126-L141 fileciteturn30file4L969-L990 At the same time, Sinemet was repeatedly recognised to improve the experience of the feet. The patient's later loss of trust should also be formulated carefully. The 2025 complaint describes a profound impact on his confidence in the clinical system, while the treating neurologist said he had not understood that the patient felt this way, described what he believed had been a very good “Tom and Liam” relationship, stated that the patient was routinely copied on his letters, and did not object to independent review or disclosure. fileciteturn31file3L110-L128 fileciteturn26file4L74-L95 March 2025 can therefore fairly be characterised as a change in the patient's understanding: severe morning symptoms were followed by marked relief after a formulation/timing change, prompting him to re-examine earlier care and ask how much of the later suffering had been avoidable. That does not prove his retrospective causal theory, but it explains the rupture without inventing longstanding hostility.

The strongest case is consequently narrower than many of the preserved formulations. It is not that Parkinsonism was invented, structural pathology was irrelevant, dystonia was obvious in 2017, Podiatry was wrong, clinicians never reconsidered the feet, or Sinemet CR retrospectively proves years of negligence. It is that a mixed foot–movement–pain problem entered Neurology together; its relationship remained unresolved; patient-specific treatment evidence subsequently showed that neurological treatment state materially altered part of that domain; later OFF, dystonia, neuropathic, structural and timing evidence repeatedly returned to it; and the eventual course included periods of severe pain, functional restriction, psychological burden and safety-significant language. The expert task is to identify the points, if any, at which reasonable care required a different degree of integration or pharmacological calibration, and then to determine whether any such departure probably caused a defined period of **additional** pain, disability or psychological harm beyond the patient's pre-existing Parkinsonism/dystonia, structural foot disease, mixed pain mechanisms and unavoidable treatment constraints.

# 11. TREATMENT-STATE / PHARMACOLOGICAL CHAIN

The pharmacological history must be separated by drug class and clinical purpose rather than compressed into “dopamine-responsive pain.”

**2017 anticholinergic phase.** Procyclidine (Kemadrin), an anticholinergic treatment in the record, was introduced slowly after rasagiline. Prof Healy later recorded dramatic motor improvement on low-dose procyclidine plus rasagiline. During a planned stop because of visual problems, the patient contemporaneously described rebuilding tension, restless feet and a walk that was “falling apart.” fileciteturn25file3L95-L101 fileciteturn20file1L70-L91 In 2018 the treating neurologist recorded rapid return of the outside-left-foot phenomenon during the withdrawal period. fileciteturn20file2L121-L128

**Dopamine-agonist phase and treatment toxicity.** Conventional levodopa/dopamine agonists had initially been deliberately deferred because of very young onset and anticipated dyskinesia/impulse-control risks. Later Neupro exposure produced genuine patient-specific chorea/dyskinesia and impulsive/compulsive concerns, making pharmacological restraint a real rather than theoretical issue. fileciteturn30file1L260-L274 fileciteturn34file0L5-L25

**Levodopa/Sinemet phase.** Sinemet was introduced by 2021. The later record shows it being adjusted according to activity and treatment state. By 2023 clinicians explicitly considered evening walking difficulty and pain in relation to OFF state and lack of levodopa; Stalevo, which contains levodopa in the treatment regimen described in the record, was used to improve movement and was perceived by the patient as helping “pep in the step” and pain. fileciteturn30file3L771-L789 Later nursing documentation described leg pain/shuffling as appearing OFF-related/undertreated and adjusted the regimen. fileciteturn22file0L9-L19

**Pregabalin phase.** Pregabalin/Lyrica was introduced for a neuropathic-pain hypothesis and escalated substantially during the severe 2023 pain state. The patient later reported that it largely removed foot pain while toe curling persisted. This is important because pain and abnormal posturing did not behave identically. By 2025 the patient had self-reduced Lyrica from 600 mg/day to 50 mg TDS, and the treating neurologist thought he might be missing its effect on the feet. fileciteturn21file4L126-L141 fileciteturn30file4L969-L990

**Morning pain and Sinemet CR.** In 2025 the patient described severe morning foot pain, first-step torture and a long overnight interval without medication. He knew that once Parkinson medication/Sinemet “hit his system” his feet felt better. The neurologist did not treat that response as proof that the underlying pain was direct Parkinsonian pain; he expressly considered pain modulation, painful dystonia, reduced pregabalin and dyskinesia constraints. Bedtime Sinemet controlled release was then introduced, and the clinician later reported that the escalation had provided profound current relief. fileciteturn30file4L979-L1002 fileciteturn33file4L105-L110 Importantly, Sinemet CR had already appeared among possible evening treatment options in 2023. fileciteturn30file3L783-L789

### What the record establishes

1. Procyclidine-era treatment materially improved the motor state.
2. Procyclidine withdrawal coincided with deterioration in feet, gait and tension.
3. Later dopaminergic treatment was clinically associated with OFF/ON differences in walking and pain experience.
4. Stalevo/Sinemet were used in relation to mobility and pain-state observations.
5. Pregabalin substantially reduced pain but did not eliminate toe curling.
6. Pregabalin was later self-reduced before renewed severe pain.
7. A long overnight medication gap was recognised in 2025.
8. Bedtime Sinemet CR was introduced for the morning state and was later reported by the clinician to have produced profound relief.
9. Dyskinesia and dopamine-related behavioural problems were genuine constraints.

### What may reasonably be inferred

At least part of the foot/lower-limb syndrome was dynamically sensitive to neurological treatment state. The later pain state was probably multi-determined: mechanical/structural pain, neuropathic or centrally modulated pain, dystonic/OFF phenomena and medication timing may all have contributed at different times. The clinical significance of benefit and toxicity had to be considered together rather than treating one side of the treatment response as more durable than the other.

### What requires expert opinion

An independent movement-disorders/pharmacology expert must determine what the anticholinergic dechallenge meant, whether the later OFF/timing pattern should have been recognised earlier, whether different levodopa timing/formulation would probably have improved function or pain at specific earlier dates, and whether that benefit would have been acceptable in light of the patient's dyskinesia and behavioural sensitivity. Pain expertise is required to distinguish pain modulation from painful dystonia, neuropathic pain and structural loading.

### What the record does NOT establish

The record does not establish that procyclidine and Sinemet acted through the same mechanism; that medication caused the underlying corns, calluses or deformity; that every foot symptom was dystonia; that levodopa response is a definitive diagnostic test for the historical pain; that Sinemet CR should necessarily have been prescribed years earlier; or that later profound relief proves all preceding severe pain was avoidable.

# 12. HARM AND CAUSATION

## Pre-existing condition

The alleged breach did not create the entire clinical problem. Longstanding foot pain, corns/calluses and structural abnormality pre-dated Neurology. The patient had undergone foot treatment since years before 2017 and had experienced pain that varied with occupational loading. He also had a real progressive movement disorder which was already causing abnormal gait, tone, bradykinesia and functional difficulty by the time of referral. fileciteturn24file1L830-L841 fileciteturn30file1L178-L229 Later PRKN testing confirmed Parkin-related Parkinsonism and dystonia, but that later result does not convert every earlier symptom into one mechanism. fileciteturn31file0L6-L19

Any damages analysis must therefore begin from a substantial non-negligent baseline: Parkinsonism/dystonia, real mechanical foot disease, existing pain and pre-existing gait/function impairment.

## Later aggravation / additional harm

What changed later was principally **severity, persistence and functional meaning**, not the mere existence of foot symptoms. The record contains long good-function periods, including very good gait in 2020 and 2.5–3 hours of daily walking in 2022. fileciteturn34file3L120-L129 fileciteturn34file2L95-L109 By 2023, however, bilateral burning pain could be extreme, debilitating and associated with “no quality of life” and death-associated language. By 2024 activities had contracted and pain and toe curling remained functionally relevant. By 2025 mornings could involve hobbling, severe pain and the first 150 steps being described as torture. fileciteturn33file2L60-L66 fileciteturn30file4L979-L1002

The potentially additional harm is therefore not “having Parkinsonism” or “having abnormal feet.” It is any **extra duration or severity** of pain, OFF disability, walking restriction, activity loss or consequential psychological distress that would probably have been avoided under reasonable alternative integrated management.

## Causal pathway alleged

The strongest supportable causal chain is:

1. An unresolved mixed structural/neurological foot–movement problem entered Neurology.
2. From 2017–18 onward, patient-specific treatment evidence showed that at least part of the foot/lower-limb state changed with neurological medication.
3. Later records added further medication-state information — morning patterns, Parkinsonian foot movement, OFF/dystonia reasoning, Stalevo/Sinemet benefit, pregabalin responsiveness and ultimately controlled-release benefit.
4. The later condition became materially more severe and restrictive at certain periods, especially 2023–25.
5. If an expert concludes that reasonable care should have altered integration, monitoring or treatment calibration at an earlier threshold, the remaining causal question is whether doing so would probably have reduced a **defined excess-harm period**.

That chain is deliberately conditional. It does not infer breach from treatment response and does not infer causation from breach.

## Counterfactual

The required counterfactual is not “What if he had never had foot pain?” It is: **What would probably have happened under the specific alternative management that an expert says was reasonable at the relevant date?**

The expert must identify:
- the first date, if any, at which care should have changed;
- the alternative assessment, referral, medication strategy or monitoring;
- the likely effect on structural, dystonic/OFF, neuropathic and pain-modulation components;
- whether any earlier benefit would have survived the patient's dyskinesia/DDS risks;
- and what period and quantum of later pain/function loss would probably have been avoided.

The record alone cannot supply those answers.

In March 2023 the nurse recorded debilitating pain and “no quality of life.” The GP recorded extreme pain “causing death wish.” In the detailed 2023 Neurology assessment the patient described the walking problem as a “heavy psychological burden,” said it “psychologically consumes him,” and described later walks as a psychological mountain. fileciteturn33file3L82-L94 fileciteturn30file3L747-L763

# 14. TRUST, RELIANCE AND RUPTURE

The record supports a relationship of continuing clinical engagement, but the patient's internal state of trust at every point cannot be directly proved.

From the patient perspective, reliance began naturally because he brought a problem he could experience but could not professionally classify to his GP, who then referred him for specialist neurological assessment. Once Neurology supplied the specialist interpretation and treatment architecture, the patient continued attending, taking and discussing medication, reporting effects, seeking advice and allowing the clinical pathway to continue. That conduct is consistent with practical reliance, though it should not be over-translated into complete agreement with every clinical proposition.

The alleged pre-discharge Protest does not necessarily contradict continued reliance. If it occurred, its content was that the feet remained unresolved, not that the patient had diagnosed himself or rejected all neurological care. Continued engagement after disagreement is entirely compatible with continuing dependence on specialist expertise. But the Protest remains disputed and should not be used to infer subjective states beyond the evidence.

The later clinical record contains positive relationship evidence. In 2023 the neurologist wrote that he and the patient had known each other a long time and “get on very well,” even while documenting a “misalignment” in how they understood the relationship between Parkinsonism and the feet. fileciteturn30file3L790-L797 In the 2025 complaint response the neurologist said he had not understood that the patient felt as he did, described what he believed was a very good, personally warm “Tom and Liam” relationship, and said the patient had been copied on his letters and that discussions largely occurred in real time. fileciteturn31file4L141-L152 fileciteturn26file4L74-L95

The patient's own 2025 complaint, by contrast, records “Loss of Trust: A profound impact on my confidence in clinical systems' ability to self-correct.” fileciteturn31file3L110-L128 March 2025 can fairly be characterised as a rupture in his understanding because severe morning pain was followed by a marked response to a new medication formulation/timing strategy, which led him to ask whether earlier suffering had been preventable. # 15. STRONGEST DEFENCE

A fair final formulation must acknowledge that the clinicians have a substantial evidence-based defence.

The July 2017 neurological assessment was detailed, not perfunctory. It documented severe Parkinsonian signs, a lower-limb posture actively distinguished from true foot drop, real pes planus/hammertoes, broad differential possibilities, a complex-case discussion, extensive investigations, allied-health input and a planned Podiatry pathway. fileciteturn30file1L178-L323 “Not clear” can therefore be defended as appropriate uncertainty in a genuinely complex mixed presentation rather than neglect.

Structural foot disease was repeatedly demonstrated by Podiatry and sometimes responded to local treatment. The patient himself had historically differentiated foot pain from leg tension. fileciteturn26file0L18-L27 This supports the contemporaneous plausibility of parallel mechanical and neurological explanations.

Specialist input was sought early. Monaghan asked Prof Healy for genetic advice, possible review and advice on the proposed levodopa restraint; Healy thought PARK II probable, recommended testing and did not direct immediate levodopa. fileciteturn25file2L33-L52 fileciteturn25file3L87-L118

Treatment restraint had a real rationale. Very young age, dyskinesia and impulse-control risks were expressly anticipated and later became patient-specific through chorea/dyskinesia and impulsive/compulsive effects. fileciteturn34file0L5-L25 Management therefore cannot fairly be tested against a counterfactual in which more dopamine had only benefits.

The course was not one of uninterrupted decline: walking could be very good in 2020 and the patient walked 2.5–3 hours/day in 2022. fileciteturn34file2L95-L109 Later care also genuinely reconsidered the feet through Parkinsonian movement classification, OFF/dystonia reasoning, repeat DaT efforts, Fabry testing, pregabalin, Stalevo and eventual CR timing. fileciteturn30file3L771-L810 Pregabalin substantially reduced pain, and the patient's later self-reduction is a material confounder in 2025.

The clinician's later response states that Sinemet benefit for pain was already recognised, that CR was intended partly to improve the morning experience, and that treatment was being limited because of dyskinesia/DDS concerns. fileciteturn33file4L100-L118 He also described extensive communication, routine copying of the patient on letters, willingness to permit independent review and disclosure, and what he believed was a positive therapeutic relationship. fileciteturn28file3L51-L76

## What survives the strongest defence

1. **The July foot–neurological relationship remained genuinely unresolved; the defence explains why, but does not convert “Not clear” into proved independence.**
2. **The 2017–18 treatment/dechallenge evidence materially changed the information state by showing treatment sensitivity in the disputed lower-limb/foot domain.**
3. **Podiatry's legitimacy does not itself answer whether Neurology retained an integration responsibility for the neurological relationship.**
4. **Later care did reconsider the problem, so the sustainable issue is adequacy and timing of integration/calibration rather than non-assessment.**
5. **Any causation case must be limited to a defined avoidable excess-harm period identified by experts after accounting for structural disease, mixed mechanisms and genuine treatment toxicity.**

# 20. FINAL EXPERT-FACING CASE STATEMENT

The patient is being referred for independent expert assessment of a longitudinal neurological and foot-pain problem beginning in 2017. The purpose is not to ask the expert to assume that the original Parkinsonian diagnosis was wrong or that all foot symptoms had one neurological cause. The record supports genuine Parkinsonism, genuine structural/mechanical foot disease and later confirmed Parkin-related Parkinsonism/dystonia. The question is how the relationship among those components should reasonably have been understood and managed as new evidence accumulated.

In April–June 2017 the patient presented to his GP with bilateral painful abnormal feet and an unusual gait. Over the following weeks the GP documented impaired initiation, increased tone, brisk reflexes, toe clawing, deteriorating dexterity and threatened work function and sought urgent neurological assessment. fileciteturn30file0L72-L105 During the July admission Neurology found marked increased tone, gross bradykinesia, a striking gait with a footdrop-like appearance described as suggestive of dystonic posture rather than true foot drop, and structural findings including pes planus and hammertoes. The record considered young-onset Parkinsonism and several alternative/genetic possibilities. It also recorded that corns, calluses and burning might be “completely separate,” perhaps neuropathic, and “Not clear,” noting that the patient himself had historically distinguished foot pain from leg tension. fileciteturn30file1L191-L253

Podiatry subsequently documented recurrent focal mechanical disease and treated it with debridement, toe props and off-loading, sometimes with benefit. fileciteturn30file2L595-L705 The independent expert is therefore asked to preserve the possibility of mixed structural and neurological mechanisms rather than assuming either exclusivity or independence.

Post-discharge treatment generated additional patient-specific information. Prof Healy considered PARK II probable and recorded dramatic motor improvement on procyclidine/rasagiline. In October 2017 a planned procyclidine withdrawal was associated with patient-reported return of tension, restless feet and gait deterioration. In 2018 the treating neurologist recorded rapid recurrence of the outside-left-foot phenomenon during withdrawal. fileciteturn25file3L87-L118 fileciteturn20file1L70-L91 fileciteturn20file2L121-L128 The expert is asked what these observations should reasonably have meant clinically and whether they required any formal re-evaluation of the previously unresolved foot–neurological relationship.

Treatment decisions were constrained by real adverse effects. Conventional dopamine replacement had initially been deliberately restrained because of very young onset and anticipated dyskinesia/impulse-control risks. Later dopamine-agonist treatment produced chorea/dyskinesia and impulsive/compulsive phenomena. fileciteturn34file0L5-L25 There were also periods of high function, including very good walking in 2020 and 2.5–3 hours of daily walking in 2022. fileciteturn34file2L95-L109

The later course nevertheless became severe. In March 2023 the GP urgently referred bilateral burning foot/lower-leg pain described as extreme and at times “causing death wish”; the Parkinson nurse separately recorded debilitating pain and no quality of life. fileciteturn33file2L60-L66 fileciteturn33file3L87-L94 Neurology later considered OFF dystonia, OFF-related amplification of pain, insufficient levodopa and alternative neuropathic explanations, investigated Fabry disease, attempted repeat DaT imaging and used Stalevo. Pregabalin substantially reduced pain while toe curling persisted. fileciteturn30file3L771-L810

By 2025 pregabalin had been self-reduced and severe morning foot pain was again dominant. The patient reported that Sinemet improved the feet after it took effect. The clinician considered reduced pregabalin, pain modulation, painful dystonia, dyskinesia risk and the long overnight medication gap and commenced bedtime Sinemet CR. The clinician later reported profound current relief. fileciteturn30file4L979-L1002 fileciteturn33file4L105-L110

The expert is asked to determine: whether the July 2017 assessment and mixed formulation were reasonable; what significance should have been attributed to subsequent treatment/dechallenge findings; whether identifiable later thresholds required different integrated assessment or medication calibration; which components of the later pain were structural, neuropathic, dystonic/OFF-related or otherwise medication-modulated; whether any reasonable alternative treatment would probably have reduced a defined period of later pain or disability despite genuine dyskinesia/behavioural risks; and what safeguarding response the 2023 death-associated/no-quality-of-life material reasonably required. No conclusion on breach or medical causation is assumed.

# 21. FINAL INTERNAL ASSESSMENT

### Strongest fact

The GP's 29 June 2017 referral transmitted a deteriorating presentation in which bilateral foot problems and abnormal gait coexisted with initiation difficulty, increased tone, brisk reflexes, toe clawing, worsening dexterity and threatened work function. fileciteturn30file0L72-L105

### Strongest inference

At least part of the foot/lower-limb experience was genuinely treatment-state sensitive, because neurological medication withdrawal and later OFF/Sinemet/CR states repeatedly changed feet, gait, movement or pain experience.

### Strongest breach theory requiring expert opinion

That an initially defensible mixed “Not clear” formulation may have required more explicit longitudinal reopening and integration once the 2017–18 treatment/dechallenge evidence and later timing/OFF phenomena accumulated, and that one or more subsequent decision points may have required different assessment or treatment calibration.

### Strongest causal theory requiring expert opinion

That earlier reasonable identification and treatment of a modifiable medication-state component — through different reassessment, dosing, timing or formulation while preserving structural/pain care — would probably have reduced a defined portion of the later severe pain and functional restriction without causing unacceptable dyskinesia or behavioural toxicity.

### Strongest defence

The patient had real Parkinsonism and real structural foot disease; July assessment was extensive; Podiatry treatment was legitimate; the patient himself initially distinguished foot pain from tension; treatment restraint had a genuine rationale later validated by dyskinesia/behavioural effects; function was often good; later clinicians genuinely reconsidered OFF/dystonia/neuropathic alternatives; and pregabalin state materially confounds the later pain course.

### Biggest factual uncertainty

Whether the alleged pre-discharge Protest occurred as recalled, what exactly was said in response and whether any further assessment, explanation or documentation followed.

### Biggest medical uncertainty

Which historical components of the foot syndrome were structural/mechanical, dystonic/OFF-related, neuropathic, centrally pain-modulated or mixed at each stage.

### Biggest causation uncertainty

From what date, if any, a reasonable alternative management strategy would probably have produced net additional benefit and how much of the later severe pain/disability would have been avoided after accounting for disease progression and treatment toxicity.

### Most important safeguarding question

What suicide/self-harm risk assessment, documentation and safety response was reasonably required by the March 2023 “death wish”/no-quality-of-life presentation, and what can reliably be established about the response that actually occurred?

### Most important proposition that must not be lost from earlier formulations

Structural pathology and neurological treatment-state dependence are **complementary, not mutually exclusive**: Podiatry can have been appropriate while the unresolved neurological relationship still required longitudinal attention.

### Most dangerous proposition to overstate

That Sinemet CR's profound 2025 benefit proves that all prior foot pain was neurological and that years of suffering were necessarily preventable.

### The single sentence I would build the eventual final formulation around

A mixed structural and neurological foot–movement–pain problem entered specialist care together, remained partly unresolved, and later became repeatedly demonstrably treatment-state sensitive, so the central expert question is whether successive new evidence required different integration or pharmacological calibration and, if so, what defined additional harm probably resulted beyond the patient's underlying disease and genuine treatment constraints.
