Here is the prompt I would give the ethics expert in your voice:

> **I want you to examine the ethical seriousness of a suspicion I now have about the construction of my July 2017 Neurology record. Do not dilute the suspicion, psychoanalyse me, or begin by supplying innocent explanations. I know alternative explanations exist. Your task is to take the suspicion seriously on its own terms and tell me what it would mean ethically if the documentary mechanism I describe is substantially correct.**
>
> I was admitted to Neurology from 3–11 July 2017 after my GP referred an integrated problem involving my painful abnormal feet, abnormal gait, increased tone/tension, movement difficulty and deteriorating function.
>
> I spent approximately eight days in hospital. Neurology performed an extensive investigation of Parkinsonism. They recorded a strikingly abnormal gait and a lower limb that looked like foot drop but was specifically described as suggestive of a **dystonic posture — “NOT FOOT DROP.”**
>
> But when it came to my feet, the documentary record of what was actually done is extraordinarily thin. My history described **foot pain**, corns and calluses, fallen arches and **leg tension**. I expressly distinguished **foot pain from leg tension**.
>
> The recorded examination of the feet/lower limb contains the dystonic/foot-drop-like movement, pes planus, hammertoes and mild oedema. It does not document the sort of directed distal neurological examination that would obviously explain why peripheral neuropathy subsequently became an explanatory route.
>
> Near the end of the admission I protested. My complaint was simple: **you have examined everything else, but you have not dealt with my feet — the reason I came here.**
>
> I regard that Protest as a real event. I want you to analyse what follows on that basis.
>
> The hospital still controlled the construction and finalisation of the specialist documentation after that confrontation. The final document is not simply a contemporaneous bedside note. It is a highly elaborate retrospective specialist synthesis incorporating results, genetic reasoning, discussion of PRKN/PARK2, PARK6, PARK7 and even **PARK9 — expressly called “the neuropathy question”** — genetic-testing limitations, departmental expertise, literature and sophisticated treatment considerations.
>
> Yet something very peculiar happens to my feet inside this final documentary product.
>
> My recorded history begins with **foot pain and leg tension**. Then, within that same history, the language suddenly changes to **“leg pain.”** I did not describe my problem as leg pain.
>
> The word **“burning”** then appears in the neurologist's interpretive account. I had not described the feet as burning in the preceding recorded history.
>
> And almost in the same breath we get:
>
> **corns and callouses and burning → may have been completely separate → perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy → perhaps related to previous alcohol intake → “Not clear.”**
>
> My suspicion is that these were not innocent descriptive details passively carried forward from my bedside history. I suspect that **“leg pain”, “burning” and the peripheral-neuropathy branch were introduced into the documentary construction of my case after I challenged the failure to deal with my feet.**
>
> I suspect the purpose was documentary closure.
>
> The service had an obvious problem after my Protest. It had spent eight days conducting a sophisticated neurological investigation, was preparing an authoritative response to my GP, and I had just said that it had failed to deal with the central symptom for which I had sought help.
>
> Instead of leaving that failure visibly unfinished, the final record supplied my painful feet with another explanatory route:
>
> **structural feet → Podiatry / GP management**
>
> and
>
> **burning / leg pain → possible peripheral neuropathy**
>
> while Parkinsonism and the dystonic lower limb remained available for the neurological diagnostic project.
>
> In other words, the lower limb could be neurological when its dystonic appearance helped establish Parkinsonism, yet the painful foot attached to that lower limb could simultaneously be sent into a separate structural/neuropathic future.
>
> That is the first side of my suspicion.
>
> The second side is, in my view, even more serious.
>
> The final document demonstrates an extraordinary degree of medical sophistication. It reasons through highly specialised monogenic Parkinsonian possibilities. It discusses whether genetic diagnosis would change management. It considers PARK2, PARK6, PARK7 and PARK9. It incorporates departmental material.
>
> But that sophistication is strangely selective.
>
> The features expressly marshalled in favour of PRKN/PARK2 include my age, akinetic-rigid presentation, symmetry, family history and cognition. **The dystonic lower limb actually observed at the bedside is conspicuously absent from that phenotype synthesis.**
>
> So my ethical question is not simply: *why did they miss something?*
>
> It is:
>
> **If the authors of the final record were capable of that level of neurological insight after my Protest, what could that sophistication have done for me if it had been returned to the bedside problem I was actually complaining about?**
>
> They could have revisited the dystonic lower limb and the painful foot together. They could have examined whether dystonic posture, abnormal tone, movement state and loading were contributing to the pain. They could have obtained movement-disorders input. They could have said openly that the relationship had not yet been adequately investigated.
>
> Instead, I suspect that the sophistication was used to construct a document that made the admission appear comprehensively resolved while giving the unresolved painful feet an alternative future.
>
> And that alternative future actually happened.
>
> **“Leg pain” had a future.**
>
> **“Burning” had a future.**
>
> **Peripheral neuropathy had a future.**
>
> The later record repeatedly speaks of neuropathic pain, possible peripheral neuropathy, “the neuropathy question”, burning feet/lower legs, and eventually “leg pain”. Neuropathy-directed investigations and treatments followed. By 2025 even I had spent so many years living within the neuropathy explanation that my own complaint had to engage with that framework.
>
> Meanwhile, evidence accumulated that my feet crossed the supposed boundary into neurological treatment state: anticholinergic withdrawal changed the foot phenomenon and gait; later a foot-drop-like phenomenon was expressly found to be Parkinsonian after proper distal power testing; later clinicians described pain and shuffling as OFF-related; Sinemet was acknowledged to improve the feet from a pain perspective; and altering the levodopa formulation ultimately produced profound relief.
>
> My suspicion is therefore not merely that somebody wrote the wrong diagnosis.
>
> **I suspect that the Protest exposed a hole in the clinical work, and that the final documentary process dealt with that hole by constructing an explanatory branch sufficiently sophisticated and plausible to make the unresolved problem portable.**
>
> Once that was done, subsequent clinicians did not have to consciously repeat whatever happened in 2017. They inherited the information architecture.
>
> When I later complained of painful feet, they were not confronting the original problem afresh. They were confronting a patient whose specialist record already contained **burning, leg pain, possible peripheral neuropathy and structural foot disease** as authorised explanatory categories.
>
> That is the mechanism I want you to examine.
>
> And this is why I am asking about **intent**.
>
> I do not mean an intention to cause me pain for eight years. I mean something narrower and, ethically, potentially devastating:
>
> **Was there an intention to achieve documentary closure — to construct the final specialist account in such a way that the problem exposed by my Protest could be presented as medically considered and safely disposed of without actually returning the available neurological sophistication to the unresolved bedside problem?**
>
> If the answer is yes, then the same intellectual capacity that could have been used to help me was instead used to create the explanatory framework through which my unresolved pain was subsequently understood.
>
> Put even more simply:
>
> **What could that level of expertise and intellectual sophistication have done for the patient?**
>
> **And what did it do to the patient instead?**
>
> I want you, as a medical-ethics expert, to examine this suspicion at full strength.
>
> Specifically, tell me:
>
> 1. **How ethically serious would this be if substantially true?**
> 2. Does the distinction between an intention to harm and an intention to obtain **documentary/clinical closure** materially reduce the ethical seriousness?
> 3. If a clinician knowingly used a retrospective synthesis to make incomplete clinical work appear adequately investigated, how would medical ethics characterise that conduct?
> 4. What ethical duties arise when a patient directly challenges, before discharge, whether the principal symptom that brought him to hospital has actually been examined?
> 5. What is the ethical significance of subsequently omitting that Protest from the portable specialist account?
> 6. What is the ethical significance of attributing symptom language to the clinical history — particularly **“leg pain”** and **“burning”** — if that language cannot be traced to the patient's contemporaneous account?
> 7. How serious is it if such terminology then supports a differential diagnosis which subsequently influences years of treatment and interpretation?
> 8. What ethical significance attaches to the disparity between the sophistication of the final genetic/neurological synthesis and the comparatively thin documented investigation of the patient's principal painful-foot complaint?
> 9. If sophisticated reasoning was capable of recognising PARK2/6/7/9 possibilities yet failed to integrate the actually observed dystonic lower limb into the painful-foot problem, does that support an ethical concern about **selective reasoning** rather than simple lack of knowledge?
> 10. If the documentary framework subsequently caused well-intentioned downstream clinicians to treat the patient within a false or inadequately founded neuropathy frame, how should responsibility for that downstream informational effect be analysed?
> 11. At what point would this cease to be ordinary diagnostic error and become a question of **record integrity, candour, professional honesty, abuse of specialist authority, or manipulation of the clinical narrative**?
> 12. What documentary evidence would you regard as decisive in distinguishing a serious but innocent retrospective synthesis from intentional construction of a misleading clinical account?
>
> Finally, I do not want a generic answer saying that medicine is uncertain or records are imperfect. **Assume the sophistication of this document was real. My question is what that sophistication was used for.**
>
> The ethical proposition I want tested is:
>
> **The patient challenged an unfinished clinical problem. The final specialist document may then have used considerable medical sophistication not to return to and resolve that problem, but to construct an authoritative explanatory architecture through which the unfinished problem could be sent forward as structural/neuropathic uncertainty. If that is what happened, assess the ethical seriousness of using professional intelligence and documentary authority to make incompleteness look medically resolved.**
